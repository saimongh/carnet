I. Life cycle of a criminal case

Your reading covered proof, not the life cycle of a case — these three are your own discussion questions. The answers below draw on general criminal-law and procedure theory rather than the excerpt.

Q1

Many crimes are never reported or prosecuted — does this matter for utilitarianism? For retributivism?

For a utilitarian, it matters quite directly. Deterrence theory works through a rough expected-cost calculation: a rational offender discounts the threatened punishment by the probability of being caught and punished at all. If huge swaths of offending are never reported or charged, that probability is low, general deterrence is diluted, and — on classic Beccaria-style reasoning — the state can only recover the deterrent effect by ratcheting up the severity of punishment for the cases it does catch, which is its own cost. Underenforcement can also blunt law's expressive or norm-shaping function: people learn, correctly, that a given crime mostly goes unpunished, which erodes voluntary compliance quite apart from any deterrence math. And because utilitarian punishment is supposed to be justified by its social benefit, resources spent prosecuting the small, arbitrary slice of crime that happens to get reported may not be the allocation that maximizes welfare.

For a retributivist, the core commitment — punishment proportionate to an individual offender's desert — is in principle untouched by what happens to everyone else. What a given defendant deserves doesn't depend on how many similarly culpable people got away with it. But this exposes a real fairness problem inside retributivism itself: its own logic demands treating like cases alike, and if punishment turns almost entirely on the accident of who got reported or caught, similarly culpable offenders end up punished wildly unequally for reasons that have nothing to do with desert. That's a genuine tension worth pressing on in class — retributivism can shrug off aggregate underenforcement more easily than utilitarianism can, but it has a harder time shrugging off the arbitrariness of who ends up in the punished slice.

Q2

If police or prosecutorial discretion should be limited, at what stage should that happen?

Discretion isn't a single choke point — it's exercised in sequence: whether police stop or investigate, whether to arrest, whether the prosecutor files charges at all (and which ones), whether to offer a plea and on what terms, and finally what the judge does at sentencing. Courts have historically been most protective of prosecutorial charging discretion specifically — it's treated as close to a core executive function and is rarely subject to judicial review (a defendant challenging a charging decision as selective or vindictive faces a steep burden). Police discretion at the stop-and-arrest stage is comparatively more constrained, at least on paper, by Fourth Amendment doctrine (reasonable suspicion, probable cause).

The harder point for discussion is that constraining discretion at one stage tends to relocate it rather than eliminate it. A mandatory-arrest policy (as in many domestic-violence statutes) removes police discretion at the arrest stage, but the prosecutor still decides whether to charge, and that decision becomes correspondingly more consequential and less visible. The same is true of mandatory-minimum sentencing statutes, which were meant to cabin judicial discretion at sentencing but are widely understood to have shifted the real bargaining power to prosecutors, who now control outcomes through the charging decision itself. So the honest answer to "at what stage" is less about picking the right stage and more about recognizing that discretion is conserved across the system — the live question is which actor you trust most (or can best hold accountable) to exercise it, not how to make it disappear.

Q3

95%+ of cases are plea bargained — is that important? Is it bad?

It's important almost by definition: if the overwhelming majority of "criminal law" as actually practiced happens in negotiation rather than at trial, then most of what a course like this teaches — burdens of proof, jury-trial rights, rules of evidence — describes the shadow those doctrines cast over bargaining, not what actually resolves most cases. Plea bargains are typically struck in the shadow of an anticipated trial outcome (a strong prosecution case commands a harsher plea; a weak one, a lighter one), so the doctrine still matters, just indirectly, as leverage rather than as the mechanism of decision itself.

Whether that's bad is genuinely contested, and reasonable people land in different places. The case against it: an innocent defendant facing a severe "trial penalty" (a much harsher sentence if convicted after trial than if he pleads) can be rationally pressured into pleading guilty regardless of actual guilt; bargaining power is wildly unequal between well-resourced prosecutors and overworked defense counsel; plea negotiations are largely invisible and unreviewable compared to public trials; and a jury — the constitutional check on prosecutorial power — never gets involved. The case for it, or at least for tolerating it: courts and public defender offices are resourced for nothing close to a world where even a fifth of cases go to trial, so wholesale abolition of plea bargaining without a matching investment in trial capacity would likely just produce worse delay and more pretrial detention; it lets both sides price in genuine uncertainty about how a trial would go; and it can produce individualized outcomes (charge or sentence concessions) that a rigid trial verdict wouldn't. Both threads are worth holding at once rather than resolving too quickly.

II. Proving crimes

A Burden of pleading

What

The obligation to raise an issue in the first place — sometimes called the burden of going forward. It takes very little evidence; it's a notice function, not a proof function.

Who

The state bears it for every element of the offense (they must appear in the charging instrument). The defendant usually bears it for issues of defense — including, notably, defenses that operate by negating an element. Mental illness offered to negate culpability still has to be raised in a timely, proper way, or the defendant may be barred from offering evidence of it later, even though it bears on an element the state must otherwise prove.

Why

Notice and administrability: the system needs to know before trial what's actually contested, and much of the factual basis for a defense (what set the defendant off, his mental state, his whereabouts) is peculiarly within the defendant's own knowledge — he's the party positioned to flag it.

B Burden of production

What

Once pleading is satisfied, this sets how much evidence a party must put on to get the issue in front of the jury at all. It's a legal threshold assessed by the judge — not a question of whether the jury will actually be convinced.

Who

The prosecution always carries it for every offense element, including when the defendant's own evidence (alibi, mistake, mental illness) tends to suggest an element is missing — the state still has to show sufficient evidence exists for that element regardless. The defendant may carry it for non-element defense issues. Many jurisdictions, following the Model Penal Code, push the production burden for most defenses onto the prosecution too, reserving the defendant's burden of production for what the Code specifically labels affirmative defenses.

Why

It's the gatekeeping function that keeps genuinely unsupported claims — on either side — away from the jury, while still letting anything a rational fact-finder could credit go forward.

Jackson v. Virginia (1979)

Provides the constitutional floor: a conviction must be reversed if, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, no rational trier of fact could have found guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. If the prosecution's evidence can't clear that bar, the case should never have gone to the jury — i.e., the prosecution failed its burden of production.

If the prosecution fails its production burden on any element, the judge enters a judgment of acquittal outright. If the prosecution has the production burden to disprove a defense and fails, the jury simply gets instructed on that defense (the judge doesn't decide it for them). If the defendant fails a production burden he carries, the defense doesn't reach the jury at all — no instruction, no consideration.

Pleading vs. production, precisely: pleading is the first procedural step — asserting that an issue exists. Production is a separate, higher bar — actually backing that assertion with evidence sufficient to justify letting the fact-finder decide it. A defendant can satisfy pleading (properly raise a defense) and still fail production (offer no real evidence, or have it undercut by the prosecution's contrary evidence) — in which case the defense never reaches the jury despite having been properly raised.

C Burden of persuasion

What

Once an issue is actually before the jury, this tells the jury which side has to convince it, and how firmly. Absent persuasion, the default finding goes to the un-burdened party.

Who

The prosecution always carries it for every element — and carries it heavily: beyond a reasonable doubt. If a reasonable doubt survives on even one element, the jury must acquit, even if it thinks that element is "probably" satisfied. The prosecution often also carries it to disprove certain defenses (justification defenses, commonly). The defendant sometimes carries it — classically for insanity — but typically at a lighter standard: preponderance of the evidence, or clear-and-convincing.

Why

It encodes the system's tolerance for error. Making the state prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt reflects a deliberate asymmetry — wrongful convictions are treated as far worse than wrongful acquittals. Where the defendant carries persuasion on a narrower defense issue, the stakes and knowledge asymmetry are judged different enough to justify a lighter standard and a different bearer.

In re Winship (1970)

The prosecution must prove beyond a reasonable doubt every fact necessary to constitute the charged crime. This is the constitutional anchor for everything below.

Persuasion vs. production, precisely: production is a legal question for the judge — "could a rational fact-finder go either way on this." Persuasion is a factual question for the jury itself — "are we actually convinced." An issue can clear the production bar easily and still be lost on persuasion if the jury simply isn't convinced to the required degree.

D Can the persuasion burden be shifted to the defendant?

Sometimes — but the line the Supreme Court has drawn turns almost entirely on how a state defines the crime, which is itself worth interrogating.

Mullaney v. Wilbur (1975)

Under Maine's murder statute, the absence of provocation was treated as part of "malice aforethought" — functionally an element. So the state could not put the burden of disproving provocation onto the defendant; that would let a defendant be convicted of murder without the state proving an element beyond a reasonable doubt, violating Winship.

Patterson v. New York (1977)

New York's "extreme emotional disturbance" doctrine covered similar ground to provocation, but New York defined it as an independent mitigating doctrine — not something that negates an offense element, but a separate reason to punish a killing as a lesser degree of murder. Because it wasn't framed as negating an element, the state could constitutionally put the burden of proving it (by preponderance) on the defendant.

Read together, Mullaney and Patterson reach opposite results for substantively similar mitigation, purely because of how each legislature chose to draft the statute — as an element (Mullaney) versus as a freestanding affirmative doctrine (Patterson). The Court has never supplied a cleaner test than that formal distinction, which is a fair target for criticism: from the defendant's perspective, the practical stakes (murder versus manslaughter-level punishment) are identical either way, so it's uncomfortable that due-process protection turns on legislative labeling rather than on what's actually at stake for the defendant.

Sandstrom v. Montana (1979)

Struck down a jury instruction that "the law presumes a person intends the ordinary consequences of his voluntary acts" as an unconstitutional mandatory presumption — it had the practical effect of shifting the persuasion burden on intent to the defendant.

Insanity has long been treated as squarely on the Patterson side of the line: states may constitutionally place the burden of persuasion for insanity on the defendant (by preponderance or clear-and-convincing evidence), because — at least where a jurisdiction defines it as an excuse rather than as negating mens rea — it more closely resembles EED than it resembles the provocation-as-malice-element structure in Mullaney.

Would we want to shift it, as a policy matter? For manslaughter-style mitigation, the case for shifting rests on administrability: the facts of provocation or emotional disturbance are largely subjective and internal to the defendant, so requiring the prosecution to disprove them on demand in every homicide risks making the exception easy to invoke opportunistically. The case against shifting is the formalism problem above — a defendant facing the functional choice between a murder and a manslaughter sentence probably shouldn't have his due-process protection depend on which side of an essentially cosmetic drafting line his state happened to land on. For insanity, the case for shifting rests on sanity being the ordinary, presumed condition of people generally, and on the defendant typically having far better access to evidence about his own mental state and history. The case against it is that the stakes (potentially lengthy institutional confinement, and a judgment about whether the defendant is a fit subject for moral blame at all) are severe enough that the same "better to risk acquitting the guilty than convicting the blameless" logic underlying Winship arguably ought to apply here too — though the Court, in Leland v. Oregon and again via Patterson, has consistently declined to extend Winship that far.

E Presumptions and inferences

Mandatory — directs (or could reasonably be read by the jury as directing) the jury to find the presumed fact once the predicate fact is proved. Unconstitutional — it functionally relieves the state of its Winship burden on an element (Sandstrom, above).
Permissive — tells the jury it may, but is not required to, find the presumed fact from the predicate fact. Constitutional so long as the presumed fact more likely than not actually follows from the predicate fact, as a matter of everyday reality — the rational-connection test.

What they actually do, evidentiarily: a valid permissive inference can serve two distinct jobs at once, which the Model Penal Code makes explicit. First, a production job — proof of the predicate fact can itself be enough to satisfy the proponent's burden of production on the presumed fact, so the issue reaches the jury at all (unless the record as a whole clearly negates the presumed fact). Second, a persuasion job — once the issue is with the jury, the court can tell the jury it's entitled to treat the predicate fact as sufficient evidence of the presumed fact, while still making clear the presumed fact must ultimately be found beyond a reasonable doubt on the whole record, not just on the inference alone.

Are these inferences actually sound?

body found in state → death occurred in state

Sensible, and low-stakes by design: it's typically used for venue, not for an offense element, so it only has to clear the lower standard venue is usually held to (preponderance), not beyond a reasonable doubt. As ordinary experience, bodies aren't usually moved far after death, so the correlation is real. The inference is sound largely because it's been aimed at a low-stakes issue — it would be much shakier if a jurisdiction tried to use it to establish a genuine element of the offense.

Sound for venue
dealer buys stolen goods in bulk, or far under value → dealer knows they're stolen

Reasonably sound, but its strength comes specifically from the "dealer" premise. A dealer has trade expertise, so red flags like a suspiciously low price or an unusual pattern of acquisition are far more diagnostic for a dealer than they would be for an ordinary one-off buyer — a naive consumer buying a single item cheaply doesn't carry nearly the same inferential weight. The inference is well-tailored precisely because it's scoped to people whose specialized knowledge makes ignorance implausible.

Sound as scoped to dealers
D knows serial number was defaced/removed → D knows item is stolen

This is the strongest of the three. Legitimately acquired goods essentially never have their serial numbers defaced — there's no innocent reason to do it — so once a defendant is shown to actually know about the defacement, the inference to guilty knowledge is well grounded in ordinary experience. Worth noting precisely what has to be proved first: not just that the item happens to be defaced, but that the defendant knew it was — the inference does its work only after that predicate is independently established.
