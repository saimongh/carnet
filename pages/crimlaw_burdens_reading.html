Burdens of Proof & Presumptions

Part II, Proving Crimes · Pleading, production & persuasion, presumptions & inferences — with class discussion notes

Criminal Law · Assigned Reading
This page expands on Part II of The Criminal Justice Process reading, which introduces the three burdens at a survey level. Everything here is rewritten in original language, not copied from any casebook or treatise; case holdings and statutory citations are reproduced because they're facts, not protected expression. Section VII below responds to my own discussion questions on this material, reconstructed in carnet's voice rather than pasted verbatim from where they were first drafted — if I want the original phrasing folded in exactly, the fix is to drop that text back in and have it merged.

I. Why “Proof” Splits Into Three

When the process reading described trial as carrying “a prosecution burden of proof beyond a reasonable doubt,” it compressed a lot of doctrine into one phrase. In practice, “burden of proof” isn't one requirement — it's three distinct burdens, each asking a different question, each allocated between prosecution and defense by different rules, and each capable of shifting in ways that change how a trial actually plays out. The burden of pleading asks who has to raise an issue in the first place. The burden of production asks whether enough evidence exists for that issue to legitimately reach the jury at all. The burden of persuasion asks how convinced the jury has to be once the issue is there. A related doctrine — presumptions and inferences — governs how a jury is allowed to reason its way from one proven fact to another. This page works through all four in the depth the survey version skipped.

II. The Burden of Pleading

The burden of pleading is the most basic of the three: the obligation to put an issue into the case formally, on the record, before either side has to prove anything about it. For every element of the charged offense, that burden sits with the state — the prosecution has to specify, in the charging document, exactly what it claims the defendant did, because a defendant is constitutionally entitled to know the accusation before defending against it. The defendant, by contrast, generally carries the burden of pleading for affirmative defenses: self-defense, duress, insanity, entrapment, and similar doctrines. If a defendant wants the jury to consider self-defense, it's the defendant — not the prosecution — who has to raise it, typically through a pretrial notice requirement or by putting on evidence at trial that fairly puts the issue in play.

This isn't just a formality. Without a pleading burden on the defense, the prosecution would have to anticipate and preemptively negate every conceivable defense just to file a valid charge, which would make charging documents unworkable in practice.

III. The Burden of Production

Once an issue has been pleaded, the burden of production asks a narrower, more mechanical question: has enough evidence actually come in for the issue to go to the jury, or is it thin enough that a judge should resolve it as a matter of law instead? For every element of the offense, the prosecution always carries this burden — and that burden persists even after a defendant raises a defense like alibi, mistake of fact, or mental illness, because those defenses typically work by negating an element the prosecution must prove in the first place. An alibi defense, for instance, isn't really a separate defense so much as a direct denial of the identity element; once it's raised, the prosecution still has to produce enough evidence on identity to survive.

The constitutional floor for how thin is too thin comes from Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307 (1979): an appellate court must reverse a conviction if, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, no rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. That's a highly deferential standard — it doesn't ask whether the reviewing judges themselves would have voted to convict, only whether some rational jury plausibly could have.

For the narrower category of defenses the Model Penal Code specifically labels “affirmative defenses” under MPC § 1.12(2)–(3) — involuntary intoxication, duress, obedience to military orders, the justification defenses, insanity, and renunciation of criminal purpose — the production burden shifts onto the defendant. Everything outside that specific, closed list stays on the prosecution. A drafter can't quietly shift the production burden onto a defendant just by calling something a “defense” in casual language; under the MPC's own framework, the burden only shifts for the defenses expressly enumerated.

IV. The Burden of Persuasion

The burden of persuasion is what most people actually mean by “proof beyond a reasonable doubt” — it measures how convinced the factfinder has to be before convicting, not merely whether some evidence exists. In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970), constitutionalized this requirement: due process forbids convicting a defendant on anything less than proof beyond a reasonable doubt as to every element of the offense.

Winship's hard edge case is what actually counts as an “element.” Because a legislature can often describe the same underlying fact either as an element the state must disprove, or as a mitigating or affirmative-defense fact the defendant must prove, the label a legislature chooses can functionally reallocate who has to convince whom of what, without changing the substance of the crime at all. Mullaney v. Wilbur, 421 U.S. 684 (1975), and Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197 (1977), show how differently this can come out on similar facts. Maine's murder statute defined malice aforethought in a way that heat-of-passion provocation directly negated, so Mullaney held the prosecution had to disprove provocation beyond a reasonable doubt — provocation wasn't a separate mitigating circumstance, it was functionally the absence of an element. New York's “extreme emotional disturbance” mitigation, by contrast, was drafted as a free-standing affirmative defense that didn't negate any element of the murder charge, so Patterson upheld New York's choice to place the burden of proving it on the defendant. The two cases reach opposite results on strikingly similar underlying facts, and the line between them — is this fact really an element in substance, or genuinely just a mitigation — remains one of the most heavily litigated interpretive questions in the area.

Insanity gets its own separate carve-out entirely. Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), and Rivera v. Delaware, 429 U.S. 877 (1976) (summarily affirming a similar rule), both allow a state to place the full burden of persuasion for insanity on the defendant, even though insanity plainly bears on culpability in a way that looks a great deal like it should be the state's burden to disprove. The doctrinal justification is that insanity, unlike Mullaney's provocation, doesn't negate any element of the crime as historically defined — a legally insane defendant still acted with the technical mens rea the offense requires; the law simply excuses that conduct separately, rather than treating the insanity as evidence the mens rea was never actually present. Whether that's a principled distinction or a convenient one is worth sitting with (see Section VII below).

The Model Penal Code takes a more categorically defendant-protective position than the Constitution actually requires: MPC § 1.12(1) puts the burden of proving every element beyond a reasonable doubt on the prosecution, and MPC § 1.13(9) defines “element” broadly enough to sweep in most of the facts that determine either guilt or the grade of the offense — leaving a legislature drafting under the MPC's own framework far less room to relabel an element as a mitigation purely to shift the burden.

V. Presumptions & Permissive Inferences

A related and easily confused doctrine governs how a judge may instruct the jury to move from a fact the prosecution has proved to a further fact it hasn't directly proved. A mandatory presumption tells the jury it must find fact B once fact A is proved — effectively removing the second fact from the jury's independent judgment and, in substance, shifting the burden of persuasion away from the prosecution. Sandstrom v. Montana, 442 U.S. 510 (1979), struck down exactly this kind of instruction, where a jury was told a person “is presumed to intend” the ordinary consequences of their voluntary acts; because intent was an element of the crime, an instruction that presumed it away from the jury's independent finding violated Winship directly.

A permissive inference is different in kind, not just in degree: it tells the jury it may, but is not required to, infer fact B from fact A, leaving the ultimate call to the jury's own judgment. County Court of Ulster County v. Allen, 442 U.S. 140 (1979), upheld a permissive inference of this kind — that occupants of a car may be presumed to possess a gun found inside it — so long as, on the facts of the specific case before the jury, the presumed fact more likely than not actually follows from the proved fact. That's a rational-connection test applied to the case at hand, not to the inference in the abstract; the same inference can be constitutional on one record and not on another. The Model Penal Code codifies the same basic standard for permissive presumptions under MPC § 1.12(5)–(6). The upshot: the exact same underlying logical move — reasoning from one fact to another — is constitutional or not almost entirely based on whether the instruction leaves the inference optional.

VI. A Sentencing Postscript

The same beyond-a-reasonable-doubt logic eventually reached sentencing. Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 U.S. 466 (2000), held that any fact — other than a prior conviction — that increases a sentence beyond the statutory maximum otherwise authorized by the jury's verdict must itself be submitted to a jury and proved beyond a reasonable doubt, not simply found by a judge at sentencing on a lower standard. United States v. Booker, 543 U.S. 220 (2005), extended that logic specifically to the federal sentencing guidelines: because the guidelines were mandatory, a judge finding sentencing facts by a mere preponderance of the evidence, in order to push a sentence above what the jury's actual verdict alone would support, violated the Sixth Amendment. Rather than strike the guidelines down outright, the Court's remedy made them advisory rather than mandatory — judges still calculate and consider the guideline range, but are no longer bound by it, which lets judge-found facts inform a sentence without constitutionally requiring jury findings or a heightened standard of proof for every fact that pushes a sentence upward.

VII. Discussion Notes — Proving Crimes

Should the persuasion burden shift for manslaughter mitigation and insanity?

Two of the doctrines above rest on a real policy choice: Patterson lets a state place the burden of disproving mitigation — extreme emotional disturbance, or heat-of-passion provocation under most modern manslaughter statutes — on the defendant, and Leland does the same for insanity. Should it be this way, or would a rule closer to Mullaney's, requiring the prosecution to disprove these facts beyond a reasonable doubt, better serve the values Winship is protecting?

The case for Patterson's approach is partly practical. Evidence bearing on a defendant's subjective emotional state at the moment of a killing often sits almost entirely in the defendant's own hands — their own account, their own witnesses, their own history with the victim. Requiring the state to affirmatively disprove that kind of claim beyond a reasonable doubt, for every homicide prosecution, risks making mitigation available almost automatically: a defendant could assert emotional disturbance, and the state would struggle to disprove a subjective mental state it has no direct access to. There's also a legislative-line-drawing argument: a state legislature that wants to define murder narrowly and treat everything else as a lesser offense subject to mitigation is doing ordinary legislative work, and Patterson gives it room to do that without every drafting choice becoming a constitutional question.

The case against it, sharpened by Justice Powell's dissent in Patterson itself, is that this reasoning proves too much. If the “real” distinction between murder and manslaughter is functionally the presence or absence of provocation — which, as a matter of substantive criminal law, it usually is — then letting a legislature relabel that distinction as a defendant's mitigation burden rather than the state's element lets it evade Winship's core protection through drafting alone. A defendant facing a murder conviction is at much greater risk from an erroneous finding than the beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard is generally understood to guard against, and that asymmetry doesn't disappear just because the state chose favorable statutory language.

Insanity complicates the picture further, because it isn't really analogous to Mullaney's provocation in the first place. A legally insane defendant typically still acted with the technical mens rea the offense requires — they intended to pull the trigger, they knew the gun was loaded — so insanity doesn't negate an element the way provocation negated Maine's malice-aforethought element. That weakens the argument for treating it as functionally an element under a Mullaney-style analysis. But it doesn't fully answer the objection, because criminal law's underlying justification for punishment is personal moral blameworthiness, not the mechanical presence of a mental-state word in a statute, and a genuinely insane defendant may lack exactly the kind of blameworthiness the whole system is supposed to require — regardless of which box the doctrine files that lack of blameworthiness into. There's no doctrinally required answer here beyond the constitutional floor Patterson and Leland set; the underlying tension — formal, label-based rules that are predictable and deferential to legislatures, against substance-based rules that are more protective of defendants but harder to administer — is one worth watching for across the rest of the semester, not just here.

Evaluating permissive inferences

The “more likely than not, on these facts” test from Ulster County is easy to state and genuinely hard to apply consistently. Three commonly tested examples, run through that test:

Possession of recently stolen property permits an inference that the possessor knew it was stolen.

Generally upheld (see Barnes v. United States, 412 U.S. 837 (1973)). Unexplained possession shortly after a theft has a genuinely strong logical connection to knowledge — most people who end up holding property stolen days ago either stole it themselves or have some reason to suspect its origin. But the qualifiers do real work: the inference weakens the further out from the theft the possession is, and it weakens further still if the defendant offers a plausible innocent explanation. This is a fact-bound inference dressed up as a categorical rule, exactly the way Ulster County contemplates.

Flight from the scene of a crime permits an inference of consciousness of guilt.

Courts allow it, but it's a much shakier inference than it sounds. Innocent people flee crime scenes for reasons that have nothing to do with guilt — fear of being wrongly blamed, distrust of police, an unrelated outstanding warrant, simple panic. Whether flight is “more likely than not” probative of guilt depends heavily on facts that rarely make it into the jury instruction itself (how soon after the crime, in what direction, toward what), which is exactly why defense counsel routinely and often successfully attacks flight instructions rather than treating them as settled.

Presence at the scene of a crime, without more, permits an inference of participation.

This one generally fails the rational-connection test standing alone. Mere presence is a weak logical predicate for guilt — bystanders are present at crimes constantly, and treating presence itself as evidence of participation would sweep in a great many people whose only fault was bad timing. Courts typically require something more — encouragement, assistance, a legal duty to intervene — before presence becomes probative of anything. An instruction that let bare presence trigger even a permissive inference of participation would likely not survive Ulster County scrutiny in most fact patterns.
