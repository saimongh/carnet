Ghen v. Rich — Case Notes

1L Property • First possession & occupancy • Custom and trade usage as law • 8-part brief

1. Citation

Ghen v. Rich, 8 F. 159 (D. Mass. 1881). United States District Court, District of Massachusetts. Opinion by Nelson, D.J. — a single-judge admiralty decision (no panel, no concurrence or dissent).

2. Procedural history

This is an original admiralty proceeding, not an appeal. Ghen filed a libel (the admiralty-court term for a civil complaint) directly in the U.S. District Court for the District of Massachusetts, seeking the value of the whale from Rich. There is no lower-court ruling to review and no procedural posture beyond "trial-level libel, decided after a hearing on the facts." Ghen is the libellant (plaintiff); Rich is the respondent (defendant).

3. Facts

•The custom. For many years, Provincetown whalemen hunting fin-back whales from open boats killed them with bomb-lances fired from special guns. A killed fin-back sinks immediately and resurfaces 1–3 days later, sometimes miles from the kill site, sometimes never at all. Each crew marks its lances distinctively, so a killed whale can be traced to its killer. The unbroken local custom: the whaler who kills the whale owns it, even after it drifts and strands; whoever finds it ashore notifies Provincetown, and the true owner comes to collect it, paying the finder a small salvage fee.
•The killing. On April 9, 1880, Ghen — a ten-year veteran of the trade — shot and instantly killed a fin-back whale near Cape Cod with a marked bomb-lance. It sank at once.
•The deviation. On April 12, the whale stranded at Brewster, 17 miles from the kill site, and was found by Ellis — not himself a whaler. Instead of notifying Provincetown as custom required, Ellis advertised the whale at auction and sold it to Rich, who stripped and tried out the oil.
•The claim. Ghen learned of the find on April 15 and immediately sent a crew member to claim it — too late; the oil was already tried out. Neither Ellis nor Rich knew Ghen specifically had killed the whale, but the court found both "knew or might have known" it had been killed by bomb-lance by someone in the whaling business.

4. Issue

Does a local, industry-specific custom — that a whaleman who mortally wounds a fin-back whale with a marked bomb-lance thereby owns it, even though it is not physically recovered until it strands days later, often far away, and is first found by someone outside the trade — operate as valid, legally enforceable property law?

5. Rule

A custom confined to a particular trade will be given the force of law, divesting the ordinary common-law requirement of physical possession, where it satisfies four conditions distilled from the whaling-possession line of cases:

1It is long-established and uniformly followed by everyone actually engaged in that trade.
2It supplies the only act of appropriation possible given the physical nature of the property and the industry — here, a killed fin-back cannot be anchored, marked-and-towed, or otherwise secured the way other whales can, because it sinks immediately.
3It is necessary to the industry's survival: without it, no one would engage in the trade if any chance finder could appropriate the fruits of a dangerous, capital-intensive hunt.
4It still rewards honest finders with salvage rather than operating as a bare confiscation of whatever a stranger happens to find.

6. Reasoning

Source of law & method

Common law, blended with custom-as-law reasoning — this is a maritime property dispute with no controlling statute or constitutional provision. Judge Nelson works through stare decisis first, then falls back on policy once precedent runs out.

He surveys three prior whaling-possession cases and finds none squarely on point: in Taber v. Jenny and Bartlett v. Budd, the whale was anchored or otherwise marked and under continuing physical control when found, so ownership followed from ordinary possession principles, not custom. Swift v. Gifford is the closest analogue — it upheld the Arctic "iron-holds-the-whale" custom precisely because a custom "embrac[ing] an entire business" and "concurred in for a long time by every one engaged in the trade" is narrow enough not to "disturb the general understanding of mankind." Since Ghen's facts fit no existing possession precedent (the whale was never anchored or marked in place — it sank and resurfaced unpredictably), Nelson extends Swift's custom-as-law reasoning to fin-back whaling, applying the four-factor logic above.

Doctrinal lineage — the whaling-possession chain

Taber v. Jenny (1856)Anchored, marked whale remains property of the first captor even after drifting from its anchorage — decided on ordinary possession principles, no custom needed.
Bartlett v. Budd (1868)Actual possession (killing + a waif/marker) vests ownership even though the whale later drifts loose and is found unmarked by another ship — again, possession, not custom, does the work.
Swift v. Gifford (1872)First case to validate custom itself as the source of the rule: the Arctic "iron-holds-the-whale" usage is upheld because it is trade-wide, long-standing, and narrow enough not to unsettle general property law.
Ghen v. Rich (1881)Extends Swift's custom-as-law logic to a harder case — the whale isn't just briefly adrift, it's genuinely lost to the first taker's control for days and found by a total outsider. Custom does the entire work here; possession principles alone would likely fail Ghen.

Swift v. Gifford — closing the gap to Ghen

Swift's facts were easier than Ghen's. In Swift, a rival whaling crew's harpoon and line struck and stayed in the whale — the iron never left the animal, only the line's attachment to the boat gave way. The whale was still under active, continuous pursuit; a second crew from the libellant's own ship simply finished the capture in the same encounter, and the two ships' masters disputed ownership on the spot. It was, in effect, an insider-versus-insider dispute over an animal that was never truly lost to the whaling fleet's control at all.

Ghen is a much harder case: the whale sank out of sight entirely, resurfaced days later and seventeen miles away, and was found not by a rival whaler but by Ellis, someone outside the trade altogether. Nelson had to stretch Swift's reasoning across three real gaps to reach his result:

1Generalizing the marking principle. Swift validated "iron holds the whale" — a physical object (the harpoon) remained lodged in the animal, giving other whalers a visible, tangible sign of prior claim. Ghen's whale carried no embedded harpoon; all that identified it as his was the bomb-lance mark and the trade's shared knowledge of whose gun-mark was whose. Nelson had to generalize from "a physical object left in the animal" to "an identifying mark plus custom-based recognition," even though Ghen had zero physical control over the whale for three days.
2The necessity/impossibility argument — new to Ghen. Nelson adds a policy justification that Swift never needed: the custom "requires ... the only act of appropriation that is possible in the nature of the case," and without it "this branch of industry must necessarily cease." Swift's whale was still physically securable by ordinary means (harpoon and line); Ghen's fin-back, by contrast, can never be anchored or towed before it sinks — bomb-lance marking is categorically the most control a whaler can ever exercise over this kind of whale. That impossibility argument is doing independent work in Ghen that has no counterpart in Swift.
3Stretching "binds insiders" into "binds outsiders." Swift was a dispute between two whaling crews — both parties were insiders who plainly knew the custom, so notice was never in question. Ghen must bind Ellis and Rich, who are outside the whaling trade entirely. Nelson bridges this gap with constructive notice: Ellis and Rich "knew or might have known" the whale had been killed by bomb-lance by someone in the business, even though neither knew the specific notify-Provincetown custom. That is a meaningfully weaker notice standard than anything Swift required, and it is the move most exposed to the outsider-fairness critique raised in the Notes and Questions.
In short: Swift gave Nelson the principle that a narrow, trade-wide custom can substitute for physical possession. It did not give him a case where the claimant had lost all physical control, where control was impossible to maintain in the first place, or where the burdened party was a stranger to the trade. Ghen supplies its own reasoning — necessity and constructive notice — to cover exactly those three gaps.

7. Holding

Holding

The Cape Cod fin-back whaling custom is valid and enforceable. Ghen owns the whale from the moment of the mortal bomb-lance wound; Ellis's failure to follow custom and Rich's purchase from him do not defeat that ownership. Ghen recovers the market value of the oil, less the cost of trying it out, plus interest from the date of conversion — $71.05, without costs.

Dicta: Nelson's aside that ordinary common-law possession principles — "if the fisherman does all that is possible to do to make the animal his own, that would seem to be sufficient" — might independently reach the same result "without regard to usage" is dicta. He explicitly declines to rest the decision on that ground: "But be that as it may, I hold the usage to be valid." Don't cite this passage as if the court held that general possession law reaches the same result — it didn't decide that question.

Stress test — hypotheticals

ASuppose Ellis were himself an experienced Provincetown whaleman who simply chose to keep the whale rather than report it. Does the custom's rationale still control? Yes, more easily — insider knowledge of the custom removes any "unfair surprise" concern, so a finder who is part of the trade has even less excuse than Ellis did.
BSuppose the whale drifted out to sea and was recovered a year later, a thousand miles away, by a commercial vessel with zero Provincetown connection. Does the custom still bind that finder? The opinion doesn't clearly say. The custom's justification — a practice "concurred in ... by every one engaged in the trade" — loses force the further the finder is from any whaling community at all. This is the genuine edge of the rule, and it's exactly the tension the Notes and Questions raise about binding true outsiders to an insider norm.

Open questions

The opinion never resolves how far constructive notice has to reach before a custom can bind a non-participant. Nelson treats Ellis and Rich's awareness that some whaling professional had shot the animal (via the visible bomb-lance wound) as sufficient — he does not require them to have known the specific notify-Provincetown custom. But the opinion doesn't address a finder who couldn't even infer that much — e.g., someone who found a beached whale with no visible sign it had been hunted at all. Treat this as genuinely open, not implicitly resolved against outsiders.

8. Concurrence / dissent

None. Single-judge admiralty opinion; no separate opinion was filed.

Cold-call ready: know the four custom-validity factors cold, know that Swift v. Gifford is the case Nelson leans on hardest, and be ready to explain why Taber and Bartlett don't actually control this case (no anchoring, no continuous possession). The insider/outsider tension in the Notes is the most likely follow-up question.
