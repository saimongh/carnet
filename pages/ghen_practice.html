Ghen v. Rich — Practice Question

1L Property • Custom as law • First possession & occupancy • Timed exam format

Fact pattern

Reyes is a longtime lobsterman working out of a small coastal town in Maine. Local lobstermen have followed an unwritten but universally observed custom for over forty years: a lobsterman who sets a trap marks its buoy with a distinctive painted color pattern registered with the town's fishing co-op. By custom, any lobsterman who comes across another's trap — even one that has drifted far from where it was set — hauls it, removes the catch, resets the trap, and either returns it or notifies the registered owner, keeping only a small "finder's" cut of the catch as compensation. No lobsterman in the area disputes this custom, and it has operated without incident for decades.

During a storm, one of Reyes's traps broke loose from its mooring and drifted twelve miles down the coast, coming to rest in an eelgrass bed within view of a public dock. Bennett, a recreational boater with no connection to the lobstering trade, spotted the painted buoy, hauled the trap purely out of curiosity, found a dozen lobsters inside, and — unaware that any custom existed — kept the entire catch and sold it to a local restaurant for $340, without attempting to identify or notify the trap's owner.

Reyes discovered the trap missing, traced it by its registered buoy pattern to the spot where Bennett had hauled it, and now sues Bennett for conversion, seeking the value of the lobsters. Bennett argues that even if lobstermen follow such a custom among themselves, it cannot bind him because he has never been part of the lobstering trade and had no way of knowing that hauling an unattended trap and keeping its contents violated any rule.

Instructions: Using Ghen v. Rich as your controlling authority, analyze (1) whether the lobstering custom is a valid, enforceable trade usage, and (2) whether it can be enforced against Bennett despite his lack of actual knowledge of the custom. Apply IRAC structure and address Bennett's outsider argument directly. Your answer should be approximately 400–600 words. Do not assume any facts not stated above.

Model answer

Issue

Whether the lobstermen's forty-year custom of returning or salvaging drifted, buoy-marked traps is a valid trade usage under Ghen v. Rich, and, if so, whether it can be enforced against Bennett — a recreational boater outside the lobstering trade who had no actual knowledge of the custom — such that his hauling and sale of Reyes's catch constitutes conversion.

Rule

Ghen v. Rich holds that a custom confined to a particular trade is valid and enforceable as law where it is (1) long-established and uniformly followed by everyone actually in the trade; (2) the only act of appropriation possible given the physical nature of the property and industry; (3) necessary to the industry's survival, since without it no one would engage in the trade if the fruits of their labor could be taken by any passerby; and (4) still compensates honest finders with salvage rather than operating as a bare confiscation. Swift v. Gifford, which Ghen relies on directly, adds that a custom "embracing an entire business" and "concurred in for a long time by every one engaged in the trade" may validly depart from ordinary possession rules precisely because it is narrow and does not "disturb the general understanding of mankind." Separately, Ghen bound the finder (Ellis) and purchaser (Rich) — neither of whom knew the whale had been killed by this specific libellant — on the ground that they "knew or might have known" it had been killed with a bomb-lance by someone in the whaling business; constructive notice of professional gear was enough, without proof either man knew the specific notify-Provincetown custom.

Analysis

Custom validity: The lobstering custom tracks Ghen's four factors closely. It is long-established and uniform (forty years, universally followed). It supplies the only appropriation possible once a trap has drifted beyond its owner's control — just as a killed fin-back whale cannot be anchored or towed before it sinks, a moored trap cannot be kept from breaking loose in a storm; the mark-and-return practice is the trade's substitute for continuous possession. It is necessary to the industry's survival: without it, any drifted trap would be fair game for wholesale scavenging, the exact "no person would engage in it" concern Ghen identified for whaling. And it rewards honest finders with a "finder's cut" rather than confiscating outright, mirroring Provincetown's salvage practice. Under Swift v. Gifford's narrowness principle — the custom touches only the lobstering trade and doesn't unsettle general property law — this is precisely the kind of usage Ghen instructs courts to enforce.

Binding Bennett, the outsider: This is the harder question, and it's the one Ghen itself leaves closest to open. Two threads favor Reyes. First, Ghen bound Ellis and Rich on constructive notice of professional gear — the visible bomb-lance wound — without requiring proof they knew the specific notification custom. Bennett is analogous: a painted, registered buoy attached to a submerged trap is a plain signal that the gear belongs to an ongoing commercial fishing operation, arguably a more obvious marker of "someone's professional property" than a whale's lance wound. Bennett didn't need to know the forty-year custom in detail to know he was taking someone else's working gear and its contents — that is a more basic notice than even Ghen required. Second, the custom gave Bennett a clear, less-culpable alternative: haul the trap, take a finder's cut, and return or report it — exactly what any lobsterman would have done, and roughly what the custom itself contemplates for anyone who happens upon a drifted trap. Instead he kept and sold the entire catch, which more closely resembles Ellis's own uncustomary conduct (selling instead of notifying) than an innocent, uninformed act.

Bennett's strongest counterargument: Unlike Ellis, who was at least embedded in Cape Cod's small maritime economy where the whaling custom's existence was plausibly common local knowledge, Bennett is a purely recreational actor with no connection to the fishing trade at all, and nothing in the facts shows the color-coded buoy system communicates ownership to true outsiders the way a name or "private property" marking might. If a custom's binding force depends on notice reasonably available to someone outside the trade, Bennett has a real argument that a forty-year intra-trade practice was never meant to reach recreational boaters who would have no occasion to learn it — precisely the "unfair surprise" concern raised in Ghen's own Notes and Questions about binding close-knit-group norms on outsiders.

Conclusion

The lobstering custom is valid under Ghen's four factors and Swift v. Gifford's narrow-trade-custom principle. Whether it binds Bennett is the closer question, but the better reading of Ghen favors Reyes: Ghen bound Ellis and Rich on constructive notice of professional gear without requiring proof of actual knowledge of the specific custom, and a registered, marked lobster trap gives Bennett at least that same level of notice. Reyes should prevail on the conversion claim, though Bennett's true-outsider argument is genuinely the strongest point on his side and is the issue a court would likely find closest.

Grading rubric — 100 points

10 pts Issue statement: Identifies both sub-questions — whether the custom is valid, and separately whether it can bind a non-participant like Bennett — rather than treating this as a single generic "who owns the lobsters" question. A vague issue earns 4 pts maximum.
20 pts Rule — Ghen's four validity factors: States all four factors accurately (long-established/uniform, only possible appropriation, necessary to survival, rewards honest finders) and connects them to Swift v. Gifford's "narrow trade custom" principle by name.
25 pts Analysis — custom validity applied: Draws the direct parallel between a drifted, marked trap and a sunk, later-stranding whale (both defeat continuous possession), and applies each of the four factors to the lobstering facts specifically rather than asserting validity in a conclusion.
25 pts Analysis — binding the outsider: Engages Ghen's constructive-notice holding (Ellis and Rich bound without knowing the specific custom) and applies it to Bennett's marked buoy, while also crediting Bennett's strongest counterargument (true outsider, no realistic notice) rather than dismissing it. Full credit requires taking both sides of this sub-issue seriously.
10 pts Conclusion and legal writing: Reaches a clear conclusion on both sub-questions. IRAC structure maintained throughout. Signal phrases used to transition from rule to application.
10 pts Citation discipline: Cites Ghen v. Rich and Swift v. Gifford by name for the specific propositions they support, rather than citing "custom law" generically.
What separates an A from a B: Most students correctly conclude the custom is valid — the four-factor parallel to whaling is fairly visible. The move that earns an A is treating the outsider question as genuinely contested rather than a formality: engaging Ghen's constructive-notice reasoning specifically, and then taking Bennett's "true outsider, no realistic notice" argument seriously as the closest issue in the case, rather than waving it away in a sentence.
Common mistakes to check against: (1) Concluding the custom is invalid simply because it's unwritten — Ghen enforces exactly this kind of unwritten trade practice. (2) Skipping the outsider sub-issue entirely and treating custom validity as the whole question. (3) Citing Ghen's dicta about ordinary possession principles ("if the fisherman does all that is possible...") as if the court actually decided the case on that ground — it didn't; it decided on custom alone.
