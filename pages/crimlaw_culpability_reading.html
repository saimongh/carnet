Culpability Requirements

Part II, Section 4 · Adapted from Robinson, Baughman & Cahill, Criminal Law: Case Studies & Controversies (6th ed.) — same sequence, original wording

This page is a rewritten, paraphrased summary of Section 4 of the casebook's mens rea materials — it is not a copy of the assigned pages, and it substitutes original wording and organization for the authors' own prose. Its narrative spine is a real, publicly reported 1999 tragedy involving an infant's death, presented here with the seriousness it deserves rather than as a device for shock value. Read the actual assigned PDF pages too — this is a study aid, not a substitute.

I. The Case of Thomas Fungwe

On the morning of Wednesday, July 14, 1999, in Southfield, Michigan, Thomas Fungwe — a 47-year-old assistant professor of Nutrition and Food Science at Wayne State University — loaded his three young sons into the family's Chevy Venture minivan. The plan was routine: drop his two older boys at summer day camp, then drive on to campus to teach. He made the stop for gas, dropped off the older boys, parked in the faculty lot, and walked into his building to begin his day. What he did not realize, and would not realize for hours, was that his five-month-old son, Thorence, was still buckled into the van.

The temperature outside climbed to 88°F that afternoon, hotter still inside a closed car, and worse yet accounting for humidity. An infant cannot regulate body heat the way an adult can. Thorence grew distressed, cried, screamed, seized, and eventually lost consciousness. A passerby noticed him motionless in the parked van and called university police. By the time he was found, he had been trapped inside for nearly five hours. Around the same time, Fungwe's wife arrived at the day camp to pick up the two older boys and realized Thorence wasn't there — she called her husband, and only then did the truth land on him. Witnesses reported he ran to the van screaming, "I killed my baby! I killed my baby!" Thorence was rushed to the hospital in severe hyperthermia; his body temperature was recorded at 108°F or higher — a police spokesman later said the thermometer used at the scene simply would not register anything higher. He died shortly after arriving at the hospital.

The casebook opens its treatment of culpability with this case precisely because it resists an easy answer. Students are asked to set aside doctrine for a moment and consult only their own intuitions of justice, using a rough 0-to-11 scale running from "no liability at all" up through "the death penalty," and to ask what result feels right for Fungwe. The exercise is designed to expose just how much of that intuition tracks a hidden judgment about his state of mind — even though every version of the story shares the identical outcome, a dead child.

The case invites arguments on both sides. In Fungwe's favor: by every account he was a devoted, previously attentive father; his behavior after learning what happened was of a man in genuine, visible anguish, not someone indifferent to his son's welfare; and the entire event traces to a single disrupted morning — a deviation from his and his wife's normal caregiving routine, not a pattern of neglect. Against him: a parent bears a basic, inescapable duty to know where an infant is at all times, precisely because an infant cannot look after itself, and the consequence of failing that duty on a summer day, in a closed car, was not some freak or unforeseeable event — it is exactly the kind of harm the duty exists to prevent.

The casebook then asks what result Michigan's actual law, as it stood in 1999, would produce, and what the Model Penal Code would say instead. Michigan's homicide statutes at the time gave students a layered menu: first-degree murder (a willful, deliberate, and premeditated killing, or a killing committed during certain other felonies); second-degree murder (essentially everything else that counts as murder); manslaughter (defined by the statute only by reference back to the common law, since the Michigan legislature never spelled out its elements); and negligent homicide (causing a death through ordinary negligence in the operation of a vehicle, on a highway or elsewhere). Layered alongside the homicide statutes was Michigan's child-abuse statute, which grades liability by degree according to both the seriousness of the harm and the actor's state of mind: first-degree child abuse (knowing or intentional infliction of serious physical or mental harm, punishable by up to 15 years); second-degree child abuse (an omission that causes serious harm, a reckless act causing serious physical harm, a knowing or intentional act likely to cause serious harm whether or not it actually does, or a knowing or intentional act that is simply cruel to a child regardless of resulting harm, punishable by up to 4 years); third-degree child abuse (knowing or intentional infliction of physical harm, a 2-year misdemeanor); and fourth-degree child abuse (an omission or a reckless act causing physical harm, a 1-year misdemeanor) — with an express carve-out preserving a parent's right to use reasonable, non-abusive discipline.

A short cluster of Michigan appellate decisions accompanies the Fungwe problem, each sharpening some piece of the line between negligence and recklessness in the homicide context. People v. Richardson, 293 N.W.2d 332 (Mich. 1980), confirms that Michigan's manslaughter statute borrows the common-law definition wholesale; the Michigan Supreme Court later described involuntary manslaughter as essentially a catch-all category — any unlawful, unintended killing that isn't murder, voluntary manslaughter, or a justified or excused homicide — in People v. Holtschlag, 684 N.W.2d 730 (Mich. 2004). People v. Ryczek, 194 N.W. 609 (Mich. 1923), held a driver grossly negligent for striking and killing a child being pushed in a cart, because he failed to give what the court called "the most casual glance ahead." People v. Clark, 556 N.W.2d 820 (Mich. 1996), defines "gross negligence" for manslaughter purposes as a three-part showing: knowledge of a situation requiring the exercise of ordinary care to avoid injury, the ability to avoid the harm by exercising that care, and a failure to exercise it when the danger was apparent to any ordinary mind. A second, unrelated case that happens to share the same party name, People v. Clark, 431 N.W.2d 88 (Mich. App. 1988), defines negligent homicide as conduct that amounts only to ordinary negligence — otherwise not a crime at all — and becomes criminal solely because a death results. And People v. Traughber, 439 N.W.2d 231 (Mich. 1989), upheld jury instructions requiring "ordinary negligence" (a want of reasonable care) as the correct standard, but still reversed the conviction, because the defendant's split-second reaction to a sudden emergency was not unreasonable once judged under the "emergency doctrine."

II. History: From One Mens Rea to Many

Criminal culpability doctrine did not arrive fully formed. Early Germanic and tribal legal systems reportedly imposed liability for causing an injury more or less strictly — you paid for the harm you caused, largely without regard to whether you meant to cause it, were merely careless, or were blameless altogether. Mens rea as a serious, load-bearing requirement grew in importance specifically as criminal law separated itself, conceptually, from the law of torts: torts exist to compensate a victim, while crimes exist to condemn and punish a wrongdoer. That distinction matters because a criminal conviction expresses society's moral condemnation in a way a civil judgment never does, and condemnation directed at someone who wasn't actually at fault is very hard to justify.

Two 19th-century House of Lords decisions illustrate early mens rea reasoning working itself out. In Regina v. Prince, 13 Cox's Crim. Cases 138 (1875), the defendant took an underage girl out of her father's custody, honestly and reasonably believing she was old enough that no offense was committed. Lord Bramwell held that general immoral conduct on the defendant's part was enough to satisfy mens rea, regardless of his reasonable factual mistake; Lord Brett disagreed, arguing the law should require intent to do something at least criminal — not merely something immoral. In Regina v. Faulkner, 13 Cox's Crim. Cases 550 (1877), a sailor stealing rum from a ship's hold accidentally set the ship on fire and destroyed it. Lords Fitzgerald and Palles held he could not be held liable for the ship's destruction — a result he never intended and could not reasonably have foreseen — simply because he had intended the lesser wrong of theft.

Over time the single, undifferentiated idea of "mens rea" splintered into multiple named categories, and the common law's vocabulary for them became notoriously unstable. "Specific intent" sometimes meant a further, additional purpose beyond the immediate act (breaking and entering with the further intent to commit a felony inside); other times it meant intent directed at a particular person or object. "General intent" sometimes meant an undirected intent to do the act itself, without more; other times it meant something closer to a general capacity for wrongdoing. The distinction was not academic — different defenses (intoxication and mistake among them) applied differently depending on which label a court attached to a given crime — even though courts, lawyers, and scholars never converged on exactly what the labels meant. Other common-law terms fared little better: words like "maliciously," "wantonly," and "feloniously" often functioned as an objective label for how blameworthy the conduct itself looked to a judge, rather than as a description of what was actually happening inside the defendant's head.

The Model Penal Code's central innovation was to abandon this vocabulary altogether in favor of what is usually called "element analysis," replacing the common law's "offense analysis." Under offense analysis, a single mens rea term (like "malice" for murder) was meant to cover an entire crime as a unit. Under the MPC's element analysis, laid out in MPC § 2.02(1), a court instead asks what level of culpability the legislature attached to each individual element of an offense — conduct, circumstance, and result elements can each carry a different required mental state, even within the same crime. This let legislatures, for the first time, write a genuinely comprehensive and precise statement of an offense's culpability requirements, rather than leaving vague common-law labels for courts to work out one case at a time (see generally Paul H. Robinson & Jane A. Grall, Element Analysis in Defining Criminal Liability: The Model Penal Code and Beyond, 35 Stan. L. Rev. 681 (1983)). The same reform also absorbed the old common-law doctrine of "mistake" directly into the culpability analysis: under MPC § 2.04(1), a factual mistake is a defense only insofar as it shows that some required culpability element is actually missing. Mistake and mens rea, on this view, are not two separate doctrines — they are two sides of the same coin.

III. The Four Culpability Levels

MPC § 2.02(2) organizes culpability into four levels — purpose, knowledge, recklessness, and negligence — ranked from most to least blameworthy. MPC § 2.02(5) makes the hierarchy operational: proof that a defendant acted with a higher level automatically satisfies any lower level the offense requires, so proof of purpose covers a knowledge requirement, proof of knowledge covers a recklessness requirement, and so on down the ladder.

Purpose

A person acts purposely with respect to a result when causing that result is their conscious object — they are actually trying to bring it about. Purpose is judged independently of how likely the actor thinks success is: someone who takes even a low-probability shot at killing another person still acts purposefully, because the law treats actually trying to cause harm as more blameworthy than merely believing the harm might happen without desiring it. That makes purpose demanding to prove at trial, since it requires evidence of what the defendant actually wanted, not just what they expected. MPC § 2.02(6) recognizes "conditional purpose" — a burglar who breaks in intending to steal valuables "if any are found" still acts purposely with respect to theft, even though the plan would be abandoned if the house turned out to be empty.

Knowledge

A person acts knowingly with respect to a result not because causing it is their conscious object, but because they are practically certain their conduct will cause it. The doctrine has to deal with defendants who deliberately avoid confirming a fact they strongly suspect, precisely to preserve deniability — "willful blindness." MPC § 2.02(7) closes that door: knowledge is established by an actor's awareness of a high probability that a fact exists, unless the actor actually believes it does not exist. United States v. Jewell, 532 F.2d 697 (9th Cir. 1976), and United States v. Hale, 857 F.3d 158 (4th Cir. 2017), are the standard illustrative "ostrich" cases — defendants who structured their own ignorance so they could later claim they didn't "know" what was obviously true.

Recklessness

A person acts recklessly with respect to a result when they consciously disregard a substantial risk that their conduct will cause it — they are aware of the risk and go ahead anyway. Awareness is the whole ballgame here: recklessness requires that the actor actually perceived the risk, not merely that a reasonable person in their position would have.

Negligence

A person acts negligently with respect to a result when they are unaware of a substantial risk their conduct will cause it, but a reasonable person in their situation should have perceived it. Criminal negligence requires a "gross deviation" from the standard of care a reasonable person would observe — something well beyond the ordinary carelessness that supports civil, tort-level negligence liability. People v. Cabrera, 887 N.E.2d 1132 (N.Y. 2008), and Santillanes v. State, 849 P.2d 358 (N.M. 1993), both police this line; Santillanes reversed a child-abuse conviction because the jury was instructed on an ordinary civil-negligence standard rather than the tougher standard criminal liability demands.

The line between recklessness and negligence is one of the most consequential distinctions in all of criminal law. A reckless actor is aware of the risk they're running, which makes them both more clearly blameworthy and, at least in theory, more responsive to the threat of punishment — the prospect of a criminal sentence can deter someone who consciously weighs a risk before taking it. A negligent actor, by contrast, is unaware of the risk by definition, which is why many scholars question whether negligence liability is either fully deserved or capable of deterring anything at all. That skepticism is part of why negligence liability in criminal law is used sparingly, often reserved for cases where death actually results, while recklessness functions as the ordinary baseline mental state for serious criminal liability. The MPC's negligence standard is also "individualized" rather than flat: it takes into account facts the actor actually knew and "the actor's situation" more broadly, which is a more forgiving inquiry than the common law's less individualized, purely external reasonable-person test. And ultimately, deciding what counts as a "substantial" risk, or what counts as a "gross" deviation from ordinary care, is not a pure question of fact for a jury to find so much as a normative, moral judgment for the jury to make. Negligence, like the other levels, applies equally to affirmative acts and to omissions: a parent who fails to get medical care for a sick child can be found to have acted purposefully, knowingly, recklessly, or merely negligently with respect to the child's resulting death, depending entirely on what that parent actually knew and believed at the time.

Faultless liability

Some offenses require no culpability at all as to one or more of their elements — strict, or "faultless," liability. Common examples include many traffic offenses, unlawful possession of certain regulated weapons (United States v. Freed, 401 U.S. 601 (1971)), and, in most American jurisdictions, the victim's age element in statutory rape (MPC § 213.6(1) reflects the same approach). Strict liability can arise in either of two ways: a legislature can simply omit any culpability term for an element altogether, or a court can hold that even a reasonable mistake about that element is no defense.

The concurrence requirement

Culpability doesn't just have to exist somewhere in the story — it has to exist at the same time as the criminal conduct itself, not before it and not only after. State v. Hopple, 357 P.2d 656 (Idaho 1960), illustrates the point directly: a defendant who forms the intent to permanently keep property only after already taking it cannot be convicted of theft, because there was no concurrence between the act of taking and the culpable intent at the moment the act occurred. Concurrence is required as to the conduct, not as to the ultimate result — a later change of heart doesn't retroactively undo liability once the culpable act and the culpable state of mind lined up.

The doctrine's outer edge shows up in the well-known "one transaction" problem posed by Thabo Meli v. The Queen, [1954] 1 W.L.R. 228 (P.C.). The defendants beat a man intending to kill him, believed him dead, and then rolled what they thought was his corpse off a cliff to stage an accident. In fact he was still alive after the beating, and it was the fall — not the beating — that actually killed him. Applied literally, strict concurrence analysis would split this into two separate events: an attempted murder (the intent was present during the beating, but no death had yet occurred) and a second, later act causing death without the intent to kill being present at that moment (since the defendants believed they were merely disposing of a body). Rather than accept that result, the Privy Council treated the entire sequence — beating and disposal — as one continuous "transaction" and upheld the murder conviction. The casebook flags this move as conceptually shaky: how far can a "single transaction" really stretch? Would it still be one transaction if the defendants had left the scene and come back two days later to dispose of the body? What if the "corpse" had actually still been alive, was later found and treated, and only died of the earlier injuries weeks afterward? Once strict, moment-by-moment concurrence gives way to a looser "transaction" theory, it becomes genuinely unclear where a principled line should be drawn.

IV. Filling the Gaps: How Culpability Requirements Are Actually Assigned

Element analysis is only useful if there's a reliable method for figuring out which culpability level attaches to which element of a given offense, especially when a statute's drafters didn't bother to spell it out for every single element. The MPC supplies two default rules for exactly this problem.

The first is MPC § 2.02(3): when an offense definition is silent as to the required culpability for a particular element, recklessness is read in by default for that element. In practice, courts applying this default to conduct elements (as opposed to circumstance or result elements) typically read in "knowing" instead, since the Code's own definition of recklessness — consciously disregarding a risk that a result will occur, or that a circumstance exists — doesn't naturally describe conduct as such; conduct is something a person does, not a risk they run. The MPC's own indecent-exposure offense shows the default rule at work across a single crime's several elements: purpose to arouse or gratify sexual desire, knowingly exposing oneself, and then recklessness (the statutory default) as to whether the circumstances make the exposure likely to affront the person who sees it.

The second default rule is MPC § 2.02(4): when an offense definition states a single culpability term without distinguishing among the offense's various elements, that term is read to apply to all of them. Applied too literally, this rule creates a real interpretive puzzle. Burglary under the MPC is defined, roughly, as entering a building with "purpose to commit a crime therein" — the only culpability term stated in the definition. Read § 2.02(4) at face value and that single "purpose" requirement would seem to govern every element of the offense, including whether the defendant was licensed or privileged to enter in the first place. Taken to its logical end, that reading would let a defendant escape burglary liability merely by hoping (rather than being certain) that they lacked permission to enter — plainly not a result any legislature actually intends.

The casebook's proposed fix reads a stated culpability term as governing only the elements within its own grammatical clause, and lets § 2.02(3)'s recklessness default fill in any elements that fall outside that clause. Applied to burglary, "purpose" governs only the intent to commit a crime once inside the building; the separate question of entry without license or privilege defaults instead to recklessness under § 2.02(3), a far more sensible result. The same interpretive move applies cleanly to the MPC's core homicide provision, § 210.1, which defines criminal homicide as purposely, knowingly, recklessly, or negligently causing the death of another human being. Read this way, the four culpability levels map directly onto the traditional degrees of homicide: purposeful and knowing killings sit at the top as murder, reckless killings occupy manslaughter, and negligent killings occupy negligent homicide — all four resting on the very same "causing death" element, distinguished only by the actor's state of mind.

It's worth being precise about what this interpretive machinery is and isn't doing. The casebook frames these proposals as solving a drafting-clarity problem, not a policy problem: the goal is to give legislatures (and the courts applying their statutes) a predictable, workable system for stating culpability requirements clearly, not to quietly raise or lower anyone's actual level of required culpability.

The excerpt assigned for this section cuts off mid-argument at roughly this point, moving next into a live debate the casebook takes up at greater length: whether there should be a culpability floor below which criminal liability simply shouldn't reach. One strand of that debate asks whether negligence-based liability can be justified at all, given the "undeterrability" concern already noted above — if punishment works by making people think twice before taking a risk they've noticed, it's not obvious what punishing someone for a risk they never noticed actually accomplishes. A second strand asks whether strict, faultless liability can ever be defended in a system that otherwise insists on personal fault; its defenders point to cases where it may be the only practical way to enforce important public-welfare regulation at scale, while its critics argue that punishing the blameless undercuts the moral credibility the criminal law depends on to distinguish itself from a mere regulatory penalty. Both questions remain genuinely open and actively argued rather than settled doctrine — a natural place to start thinking as the semester turns from these general principles toward the substantive offenses that put them to work.
