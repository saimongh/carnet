Fact pattern

Dana was walking on the public sidewalk directly beneath a scaffold erected around a five-story building undergoing facade renovation. The renovation is being performed exclusively by Ridgeline Restoration Co. under a contract with the building's owner; no other contractor or member of the public has been granted access to the scaffold or the floors it serves.

As Dana passed beneath the scaffold, a metal bracket fell from somewhere above and struck her shoulder, causing a fracture. No Ridgeline employee saw the bracket fall, and no witness can say exactly how it came loose, from which level it fell, or which worker (if any) was handling it at the time.

Dana sues Ridgeline for negligence. She has no direct evidence of any specific act or omission — no testimony that a particular worker dropped the bracket, left it balanced improperly, or failed to secure it. Ridgeline's foreman testifies that brackets of that type are used only by Ridgeline crews, are stored in sealed bins when not in use, and that Ridgeline conducts a nightly sweep of the scaffold to secure loose materials.

Ridgeline argues that Dana cannot win without producing evidence of a specific negligent act, and that on this record a jury would have to guess at what went wrong. Ridgeline also argues that because the bracket could theoretically have been dislodged by a sudden gust of wind — an event outside Ridgeline's control — the necessary link between its conduct and the injury has not been shown.

Instructions: Using Byrne v. Boadle as your controlling authority, analyze whether Dana can establish Ridgeline's negligence without direct evidence of a specific negligent act. Apply IRAC structure. Address both of Ridgeline's arguments directly and separately. Your answer should be approximately 350–550 words. Do not assume any facts not stated above.

Model answer

Issue

Whether Dana can establish that Ridgeline was negligent when a bracket fell from Ridgeline's exclusively-controlled scaffold and injured her, despite her inability to identify the specific negligent act or the worker responsible.

Rule

Ordinarily, a negligence plaintiff must prove duty, breach, causation, and damages with evidence of the defendant's specific conduct. Res ipsa loquitur — "the thing speaks for itself" — permits a jury to infer negligence without such direct evidence when the accident is of a kind that ordinarily does not occur absent negligence, and the instrumentality causing the harm was within the defendant's exclusive control.

Byrne v. Boadle established the doctrine on nearly identical logic: a barrel does not roll out of a second-story warehouse window and fall on a passerby unless someone was careless. The court held that the plaintiff need not show the specific act of negligence — the circumstances of the accident itself permit the inference. Exclusive control of the premises and instrumentality by the defendant was central to that inference; it is what rules out other likely explanations for the accident.

Analysis

Ridgeline's first argument — no evidence of a specific act: This is precisely the argument Byrne rejects. The plaintiff in Byrne likewise had no witness to the specific negligent act — no one saw how or why the barrel came to roll off the ledge. The court held that the accident's very nature supplied the missing evidence: barrels do not ordinarily roll out of warehouse windows absent carelessness in their storage or handling. The same logic applies here. Metal brackets used in scaffold construction do not ordinarily detach and fall onto the sidewalk below absent some failure to secure them properly. Dana does not need a witness to the specific dropped bracket any more than the Byrne plaintiff needed a witness to the specific rolling barrel.

Exclusive control: The exclusive-control element is satisfied on these facts, arguably more cleanly than in Byrne itself. Ridgeline is the sole contractor with access to the scaffold; no other party — contractor or member of the public — had any opportunity to handle materials at height. Ridgeline's own foreman confirms this: only Ridgeline crews use this type of bracket, and Ridgeline controls their storage. This forecloses the inference that some third party's conduct, rather than Ridgeline's, caused the fall.

Ridgeline's second argument — a gust of wind: This argument does not defeat res ipsa; at most it goes to the weight of the inference, not its availability. Res ipsa does not require the plaintiff to rule out every conceivable alternative cause — it requires that the accident be of a type that does not ordinarily occur without negligence, given the totality of circumstances. A properly secured bracket, stored or affixed in accordance with reasonable scaffold safety practice, does not become airborne and fall five stories in ordinary wind. If wind alone were sufficient to dislodge unsecured materials at height, that itself suggests inadequate securing — which is a negligence question for the jury, not a basis to dismiss the case as a matter of law. Ridgeline is free to argue the wind theory to the jury, but it does not eliminate the res ipsa inference at the threshold.

Conclusion

Dana can establish a prima facie case of negligence under res ipsa loquitur. A bracket falling from an exclusively-controlled scaffold is the kind of accident that ordinarily does not occur absent negligence, and Ridgeline's exclusive control of the scaffold and its materials satisfies the second element. Ridgeline's inability-to-identify-the-specific-act argument is the same argument Byrne rejected, and its wind theory is a factual dispute for the jury rather than a bar to the inference.

Grading rubric — 100 points

10 pts Issue statement: Identifies that the question is whether negligence can be inferred without direct evidence of a specific act — not simply "was Ridgeline negligent." A vague issue earns 4 pts maximum.
20 pts Rule — res ipsa loquitur elements: States both elements accurately: (1) the accident is of a type that ordinarily does not occur without negligence, and (2) the instrumentality was within the defendant's exclusive control. Must connect this to Byrne's barrel-and-warehouse reasoning specifically, not just a generic negligence rule.
20 pts Analysis — no specific act required: Directly parallels Dana's situation to Byrne's — no witness to the specific negligent act in either case — and explains why the nature of the accident itself supplies the inference.
20 pts Analysis — exclusive control: Applies the exclusive-control element to the facts (sole contractor, no third-party access, controlled storage) and explains why this forecloses alternative explanations.
15 pts Analysis — the wind argument: Correctly treats the wind theory as a factual dispute for the jury rather than a threshold bar to res ipsa, and explains why an object dislodged by ordinary wind suggests inadequate securing rather than an act of nature.
10 pts Conclusion and legal writing: Conclusion directly answers the issue. IRAC structure maintained throughout. Signal phrases used to transition from rule to application.
5 pts Citation discipline: Cites Byrne v. Boadle specifically for the res ipsa inference rather than citing negligence doctrine generically.
What separates an A from a B: Most students correctly spot res ipsa. The move that earns an A is engaging seriously with Ridgeline's wind argument instead of dismissing it — recognizing that res ipsa doesn't require eliminating every alternative cause, only showing the accident type ordinarily implies negligence, and that the alternative-cause argument goes to the jury's weighing, not the threshold question.
Common mistakes to check against: (1) Treating the lack of a witness to the specific act as fatal to Dana's case — that is exactly the fact pattern Byrne was decided to solve. (2) Skipping the exclusive-control element entirely and only discussing "accidents like this don't normally happen." (3) Conceding the case to Ridgeline because of the wind theory rather than explaining why it doesn't defeat the inference at this stage.
