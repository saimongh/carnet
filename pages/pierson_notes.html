Pierson v. Post — Case Notes

1L Property • First possession & occupancy • Wild animals (feræ naturæ) • 8-part brief

1. Citation

Pierson v. Post, 3 Cai. R. 175 (N.Y. Sup. Ct. 1805). Supreme Court of New York, August Term. Majority opinion by Tompkins, J. Dissent by Livingston, J.

2. Procedural history

Post sued Pierson in a justice's court for trespass on the case. The jury returned a verdict for Post (the plaintiff below). Pierson sued out a certiorari to bring the record up to the New York Supreme Court, assigning as error that the facts stated in the declaration were not legally sufficient to maintain the action — i.e., even taking Post's version of events as true, no cause of action existed. This is thus a review of a lower-court judgment on a pure question of law, not a retrial of disputed facts.

3. Facts

•The hunt. Post, with his own dogs and hounds, found and started a fox on wild, uninhabited, unpossessed waste land ("the beach"), and was hunting, chasing, and pursuing it with his hounds.
•The interception. Pierson, knowing full well that the fox was being hunted and pursued by Post, and in Post's sight, killed the fox and carried it off — specifically to prevent Post from catching it.
•What's absent. Nothing in the declaration alleges that Post ever wounded the fox, touched it, trapped it, or otherwise physically restrained it before Pierson's interception. The entire dispute is over pursuit alone, unaccompanied by any act of physical control.

4. Issue

Does sustained, intentional pursuit of a wild animal — finding it, starting it, and chasing it with hounds, without any mortal wounding, capture, or other act depriving it of its natural liberty — confer on the pursuer a property right sufficient to sustain an action against a rival hunter who, aware of the chase, intercepts, kills, and takes the animal first?

5. Rule

Property in animals feræ naturæ (wild by nature) is acquired by occupancy, and by occupancy alone — there is no other recognized mode of first acquiring title to an unowned wild creature. The majority defines occupancy narrowly:

1Actual corporal possession (manucaption) is sufficient but not the only route.
2Mortal wounding by a pursuer who does not abandon the chase also counts, because it manifests an unequivocal intent to appropriate the animal and deprives it of its natural liberty.
3Nets, traps, or snares that circumvent the animal and render escape impossible likewise establish possession, even without a physical touch.
4Mere pursuit — however prolonged, skillful, or costly — is categorically insufficient. Natural liberty is not lost merely because an animal is chased, even in plain sight of the pursuer.

6. Reasoning

Source of law & method

There is no controlling statute or prior American precedent on point — Tompkins expressly calls this "a novel and nice question." With no binding authority to apply, the court turns to civil-law and natural-law treatise writers as persuasive authority on first principles: Justinian's Institutes (pursuit alone vests no right, even pursuit plus wounding, unless the animal is actually taken), Puffendorf (occupancy requires actual corporal possession), and Barbeyrac's notes on Puffendorf (allowing mortal wounding or net/snare confinement to substitute for bodily seizure, since either manifests an unequivocal intent to appropriate and removes the animal's natural liberty).

Tompkins adopts Barbeyrac's more generous standard (mortal wounding or equivalent confinement counts) but still requires some act beyond pursuit. Having found the authorities "decisive" that mere pursuit is not enough, the court then gives an independent policy justification: a rule turning on pursuit alone, rather than a bright-line act of control, "would prove a fertile source of quarrels and litigation," because pursuit is inherently a matter of degree and proof, while capture (or its equivalent) is a hard, verifiable line. Certainty and social peace, not just doctrinal fidelity to the treatises, drive the result.

Majority vs. dissent — two competing tests for occupancy

Tompkins majorityOccupancy requires an act that actually deprives the animal of its natural liberty and brings it within the pursuer's certain control — mortal wounding, capture, or net/trap confinement. A bright-line, verifiable standard chosen explicitly for certainty and to prevent hunters' quarrels from becoming litigation.
Livingston dissentOccupancy should not require bodily touch or mortal wounding; it is enough that the pursuer is within reach or has a reasonable prospect of taking the animal, given the pursuit already undertaken. Motivated by protecting the labor and investment of the "first" hunter and by the social value of destroying "noxious beasts" like foxes — concerns the majority's stricter rule does not weigh at all.

7. Holding

Holding

Pursuit alone, without mortal wounding, capture, or equivalent physical control, gives no right of property in a wild animal. Post's declaration, even taken as true, alleges nothing more than pursuit, so it fails to state a legal injury. The judgment below (for Post) is reversed; Pierson, who killed and took the fox first, is not liable.

Note on scope: The majority's rule is narrower than it might first appear — it does not hold that pursuit is worthless, only that pursuit unaccompanied by any act of control (wounding, trapping, seizing) is legally insufficient. A pursuer who wounds an animal so severely that it can no longer meaningfully evade capture is treated the same as one who has physically seized it.

Stress test — hypotheticals

ASuppose Post's hounds had cornered the fox against a cliff face with no possible escape route, but no hound had yet bitten it, when Pierson shot it. Does the majority rule protect Post? Probably yes — cornering an animal so its escape is rendered impossible looks functionally identical to the net/snare confinement Barbeyrac and the majority treat as sufficient, even without a physical touch.
BSuppose Pierson had no idea Post was hunting the fox at all — he simply happened upon it independently and killed it. Would the result change? No — Pierson's knowledge of Post's pursuit is not part of the majority's rule at all; it matters only rhetorically ("however uncourteous or unkind"). The rule turns entirely on whether Post ever established occupancy, not on Pierson's state of mind. This is a useful contrast with Ghen v. Rich, where the outsider's knowledge (constructive notice) does the crucial work — Pierson has no custom to enforce and so no notice inquiry at all.

Open questions

The majority never says exactly how severe a wound must be to count as "mortal wounding" short of an actual kill, nor precisely how much confinement a net or trap must impose. The opinion resolves the extremes (mere pursuit fails; capture succeeds) but leaves the middle range — a wound that will eventually prove fatal but leaves the animal capable of fleeing for now — genuinely open. This is exactly the ambiguity the fact pattern in pierson_practice.html is built to probe.

8. Concurrence / dissent

Livingston, J., dissented. He would have affirmed the judgment for Post. His opinion rejects the majority's insistence on strict corporal possession or mortal wounding, arguing it is enough that the pursuer has a reasonable prospect of taking the animal given the pursuit already undertaken — here, in his view, plainly satisfied by a sustained, committed chase that "must inevitably and speedily have terminated in corporal possession" absent Pierson's interference. Livingston also brings in policy grounds absent from the majority: rewarding the labor and skill invested in the chase, discouraging a "saucy intruder" from free-riding on another's effort, and encouraging the destruction of foxes as pests beneficial to farmers. Notably, Livingston reaches for policy first and treatise authority second — almost the reverse emphasis of Tompkins's opinion.

Cold-call ready: know precisely where the majority draws the line (mortal wounding / net-trap confinement = occupancy; pursuit alone, however sustained = not occupancy), know that Livingston's dissent turns on a "reasonable prospect of taking" standard rather than actual control, and be ready to explain why the majority prefers a bright-line rule (certainty, avoiding litigation) over Livingston's fact-sensitive one. The wounded-but-still-fleeing hypothetical is the natural follow-up question.
