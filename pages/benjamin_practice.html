Benjamin v. Lindner Aviation — Practice Question

1L Property • Found property • Mislaid vs. lost vs. abandoned vs. treasure trove • Timed exam format

Fact pattern

Priya buys a secondhand file cabinet at an estate sale. Six months later, while cleaning it out, she finds a manila envelope taped to the underside of the bottom drawer, hidden beneath the drawer's liner. Inside is $4,000 in cash, bundled with a rubber band, mixing bills from three different decades — the newest dated eight years ago.

The estate sale was run by the deceased owner's adult children, who inherited the cabinet along with the rest of the house's contents and sold it without opening the drawers. No notice of the discovery has been given to anyone, and Priya has not attempted to locate or notify the heirs.

Priya wants to know whether the money is legally hers. The heirs, once they learn of the discovery, claim it belongs to the estate.

Instructions: Using Benjamin v. Lindner Aviation as your controlling authority, classify the money under the common law's four categories of found property and analyze who has the superior claim to it. Apply IRAC structure, and address the strongest counterargument on the other side. Your answer should be approximately 400–600 words. Do not assume any facts not stated above.

Model answer

Issue

Whether cash discovered concealed inside a purchased piece of furniture belongs to the finder (Priya), to the current owner of the article in which it was concealed (the estate/heirs), or to neither — turning on whether the money is classified under the common law as lost, mislaid, abandoned, or treasure trove property.

Rule

Absent an applicable lost-property statute reaching this kind of find, Benjamin applies four common-law categories. Abandoned property: the owner intends to abandon it and has voluntarily relinquished all right, title, and interest — belongs to the finder against everyone, including the former owner. Lost property: the owner unintentionally and involuntarily parts with possession and does not know where it is. Mislaid property: the owner voluntarily puts it in a particular place and then overlooks or forgets where it is — the finder acquires no rights, and possession belongs instead to the owner of the "premises" where it was found, as against all but the true owner. Treasure trove: concealed coins or currency with an element of antiquity, hidden long enough that the true owner is probably dead or undiscoverable — belongs to the finder against all but the true owner.

Benjamin instructs that the manner and place of concealment are the key evidence for sorting a find into one of these categories, and that classification is a fact question reviewed for substantial evidence.

Analysis

Mislaid is the strongest characterization. In Benjamin, currency carefully tied, wrapped in foil and handkerchiefs, and hidden behind a screwed-down panel supported an inference of intentional concealment — evidence the owner meant to retain and later reclaim the property, which is fatal to both a "lost" and an "abandoned" finding. The envelope here is materially the same kind of evidence: taped deliberately to the underside of a drawer, beneath the liner, bundled with a rubber band. That is not the profile of something someone unintentionally and involuntarily parted with (ruling out "lost"), and Benjamin holds that deliberate, careful concealment cuts directly against a finding of abandonment too.

Who owns the "premises." Benjamin holds that mislaid property belongs to the owner of the premises where it is found — and specifically holds that the "premises" is the object actually concealing the property (the airplane), not the larger space it happened to be sitting in (the hangar). By that logic, the relevant "premises" here is the file cabinet itself, or arguably the house/estate it came from, not just wherever Priya happened to bring it home. Because the cabinet passed from the decedent's estate to the heirs and then to Priya by sale, the strongest reading is that the heirs — as the party in the chain of title before Priya, and the party positioned to represent the decedent's (likely) ownership of the cash — have the superior claim to mislaid property, not Priya as finder.

Treasure trove is weaker than it looks. Priya may argue treasure trove, given that currency (not an ordinary lost item) was concealed. But Benjamin requires "an element of antiquity" sufficient that the true owner is probably dead or undiscoverable, and in Benjamin itself thirty-five-year-old bills with no evidence of the plane's inspection history was not enough to require a treasure-trove finding as a matter of law. Eight-year-old currency is far short of that, and — unlike in Benjamin, where the true owner was genuinely unknown — the likely owner here (the decedent) is identifiable, and her heirs are known and available. That undercuts the "undiscoverable owner" element treasure trove requires.

Strongest counterargument (abandonment). Priya could argue, as Justice Snell did in dissent, that the money was effectively abandoned — the family cleared out the entire house without discovering it, sold the cabinet without inspecting the drawers, and (unlike Benjamin) no one has given the kind of public notice chapter 644 required in Iowa. But Snell's dissent leaned specifically on the fact that Benjamin had given legally required public notice and no owner claimed the money within the statutory window — that is what let him say the owner's silence, after being told the money existed, amounted to relinquishment. Nothing in this fact pattern shows equivalent notice was ever given here; the heirs simply never knew to look. That gap makes the abandonment argument considerably weaker than Snell's.

Conclusion

The money is most likely mislaid property under Benjamin. Because mislaid property belongs to the owner of the "premises" where it was found — here, the cabinet, tracing back through the estate — the heirs have the superior claim, not Priya. A treasure-trove or abandonment argument for Priya is not frivolous, but both are weaker here than the closest analogues in Benjamin and its dissent, given the shorter concealment period, the identifiable true owner, and the absence of any public notice process.

Grading rubric — 100 points

10 pts Issue statement: Frames the question as a classification-and-ownership dispute among the four Benjamin categories, naming Priya and the heirs as the competing claimants. A vague issue ("Is this Priya's money?") earns 4 pts maximum.
20 pts Rule — all four categories stated accurately: States abandoned, lost, mislaid, and treasure trove correctly, including who each category benefits (finder vs. owner of premises) and the antiquity requirement for treasure trove.
25 pts Analysis — mislaid classification: Draws the concealment-method analogy to Benjamin's foil-and-handkerchief packets, and correctly applies the "premises" holding (the cabinet, not wherever Priya took it) to conclude the heirs — not Priya — have the stronger claim if the property is mislaid.
15 pts Analysis — treasure trove considered and rejected: Recognizes the antiquity requirement, compares the eight-year timeline against Benjamin's own inconclusive thirty-five-year discussion, and notes the true owner here is identifiable through the heirs.
20 pts Counterargument — abandonment via the dissent: Engages Justice Snell's dissent specifically, and identifies that Snell's reasoning depended on legally required public notice going unanswered — a fact missing here — as the key distinguishing weakness in an abandonment claim for Priya.
10 pts Conclusion and legal writing: Conclusion directly answers who prevails. IRAC structure maintained throughout. Clear, committed position rather than excessive hedging, while still acknowledging genuine uncertainty where it exists (treasure trove, abandonment).
What separates an A from a B: Most students correctly spot "mislaid" as the best-fitting category. The move that earns an A is catching Benjamin's "premises" holding and applying it correctly — recognizing that mislaid property doesn't go to the finder at all, and that the relevant premises is the cabinet (tracing to the estate), not just "wherever Priya happened to have it."
Common mistakes to check against: (1) Concluding the money is Priya's simply because she found it — under a mislaid finding, the finder gets nothing. (2) Treating "treasure trove" as automatic whenever old cash is found, without checking the antiquity/undiscoverable-owner element against the facts. (3) Citing the dissent for abandonment without noticing that Snell's reasoning specifically depended on public notice having been given and gone unanswered — a fact this hypothetical doesn't include.
